This demonstration appears to be based on a passage from Aristotle's Physics, which may be summarized as follows: Three things may be considered in motion: the thing moved, the mover, and that by which the latter moves. That which is moved does not necessarily communicate motion; that which serves as an instrument or intermediary communicates motion at the same time as it receives it; finally, that which moves without being an instrument or intermediary is itself immobile. Now, since we see (in the universe), on the one hand, that which is moved without having within itself the principle of motion—that is, without moving anything else—and on the other hand, that which is simultaneously moved by another and the mover of another, it is reasonable, if not necessary, to admit a third thing which moves without being moved. See Phys., Book VIII, ch. 5 (Bekker ed., p. 256 b): τρία γὰρ ἀνάγκη εἶναι, τό τε κινουμένον, καὶ τὸ κινοῦν, καὶ τὸ ᾧ κινεῖ. κ. τ. λ. In the Metaphysics, Book XII, chap. VII, Aristotle summarizes himself in these terms: ἔστι τοίνυν τι καὶ ὃ κινεῖ, ἐπεὶ δὲ τὸ κινούμενον καὶ κινοῦν, καὶ μέσοντοίνυν ἐστί τι ὃ οὐ κινούμενον κινεῖ, ἀΐδιον καὶ οὐσία καὶ ἐνέργεια οὖσα. Alexander of Aphrodisias explained this passage in nearly the same terms used by Maimonides in this second demonstration; and it is evidently from Alexander that our author borrowed his argumentation, as well as the proposition he places at the head as having been stated by Aristotle himself. Alexander's explanation was cited by Averroes in his large commentary on the Metaphysics. We reproduce it from the Latin version of this commentary (folio ed., f. 149 verso): "Alexander said: This is the reason why [there is] some mover [which] is not moved, and it is stated briefly and as a reminder of what was said in the last [book] of the Physics. And it is founded upon two propositions, one of which is that every composite of two things, of which one can exist by itself, the other will also possibly exist by itself, unless the composition be of substance and accident; for example, because hydromel is composed of water and honey, and honey is found by itself, it is therefore necessary that water be found by itself. And, because we find something moved and moving as if composed of a mover and a moved, and we find something moved by itself and not moving, it is manifest that it is necessary for there to be some mover and not moved."
